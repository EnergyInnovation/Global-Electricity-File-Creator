Supplementary Note 1: Physical models 
Physical models are based on theories of heat transfer and thermal diffusion and use energy 
balance equations to simulate a building’s energy demand. They vary in complexity from 
simplified resistance-capacitance models [1,2] to highly detailed models implemented in a 
variety of purpose -built software (e.g. EnergyPlus [3]). The sheer variation in the built 
environment and users’ interactions with it means that many inputs must be specified (10s 
to 100s) to simulate heating and cooling loads using physical models [4]. These inputs 
include physical parameters (e.g. envelope characteristics and HVAC equipment), user 
behaviours (e.g. occupancy patterns) and local meteorological conditions (e.g. air 
temperature and solar irradiance) [4–6].  
 
Physical models are ubiquitous in the building industry, particularly in the conceptual design 
stages where building design is most flexible, because their detailed parametric approach 
allows the user to customise a very wide range of input parameters to create bespoke 
estimates of the energy consumption of different building designs. However, such a detailed 
approach limits the sphere of application of physical models.  Where a detailed description 
of an entire stock of buildings is non -existent, the distribution of building characteristics 
must be assumed based on the available data [7]. While these generic inputs may effectively 
describe the building stock in a single neighbourhood or city, they could not be used to 
describe the stock of another city. As parameters are manually defined and simulation run-
times ar e long, each simulation is also very time -consuming [4]. Consequently, physical 
models are typically applied only to individual buildings, neighbourhoods or (if sufficiently 
generalised) cities [4,5,8]. While there are many studies that validate the outputs of physical 
models against measured energy consumption, there is growing body of evidence that 
shows significant discrepancies between simulated and measured energy consumption due 
to mis -calibration [9–14]. The causes of such deviations include in accurate assumptions 
regarding building design, user behaviours and occupancy patterns [12,15–17].

Supplementary Note 2: Statistical models 
Statistical models extract mathematical relationships between measurements of energy 
consumption and other variables, which are then applied to predict energy consumption in 
the unknown future or in new types of building. A wide variety of statistical methods are 
available which can be broadly categorised as regression models [18,19] and machine 
learning models [20,21]. The variables or ‘features’ that are used to predict demand in the 
model are selected based on their predictive power for energy consumption, although 
feature selection is ultimately limited by availability of data and processing time [22–24]. 
The number of features included varies substantially between models, but is typically less 
than 10 [24]. While some use only temperature to predict energy consumption, others 
include additional meteorological variables (e.g. solar irradiance, wind speed and humidity) 
and physical building characteristics (e.g. insulation, glazed area, type of roofing) [24,25]. A 
key advantage of statistical models is that, where suitable data exist, models can give fast 
predictions of energy consumption in known buildings, and because the models are derived 
from measured energy data, they are effectively self-validating – at least within their 
sample data.  
 
The drawback with statistical models is that it is hard to use their results to inform modelling 
in new contexts. The high cost of data collection means statistical models are often derived 
from small underlying datasets, which has two important implications: 1) models based on 
small samples are often less accurate when predicting out-of-sample (i.e. in new buildings), 
and 2) in models with many predictors there is a risk of overfitting and producing misleading 
results [23]. As is manifested in physical models, building energy consumption is influenced 
by tens or even hundreds of possible features and interactions, which vary by region, culture 
and so on [5]. As a result, it is difficult to generalise any one statistical model to fit another 
geographical location or customise it to understand a new context. Even if it were 
appropriate to generalise or customise statistical models, the model could only be applied 
to contexts where corresponding data exist.

Supplementary Note 3: Surrogate models 
Surrogate models are used to emulate building simulation software (e.g. EnergyPlus) by 
training a statistical model on simu lation input (i.e. weather files and building descriptors) 
and output data (e.g. heating and cooling demand) [26]. Once trained, evaluating the 
surrogate model is many orders of magnitude faster than running detailed physical 
simulations, thereby reducin g the computational barrier to building energy simulation 
[26,27]. Because their training sample is derived using building simulation software, they do 
not require measured data to derive a statistical model – this is particularly useful when 
comparing c onceptual building designs and for optimising design features [26]. Most 
surrogates have been trained for specific building projects [28–30] or specific climate zones 
[31–33], but some surrogates have been trained to cater for a variety of building t ypes 
across multiple climate zones (e.g. for all climate zones in Morocco [34], Brazil [35] and 
Canada [36]). The later study [36] is of particular interest as it demonstrates that surrogates 
can be used to simulate hourly energy demand – as opposed to annual energy demand – 
albeit with greater error.  
 
With a sufficiently large and generalised training set, a single surrogate model could in 
theory (though not yet in practice) be used to simulate temporally-resolved energy demand 
across an exhaustive array of building designs and climate zones [26,36]. Such a model 
would be both generalisable and customisable. Depending on the setup  (i.e. whether it 
requires a comprehensive or more generic description of the building design), the model 
could also be applied with widely available (or entirely conceptual) data. The key problem 
with surrogate models is that they are trained using buildi ng simulations, so they are only 
accurate within the limitations of the simulation program and the input data that are used 
[26]. While surrogate models often show a high -degree of accuracy in emulating physical 
models, evidence suggests that the outputs of physical models can deviate significantly from 
measured data [9–14]. For example, Catalina et al. [31] find that their surrogate model’s 
predictions are within 1% of simulated demand, but are nearly 40% higher than measured 
demand (before applying a building specific adjustment factor).

Supplementary Note 4: Degree-day models 
In contrast to other approaches, degree -day methods are designed to spatially and 
temporally resolve widely -available but highly aggregated measurements of energy 
demand in buildings, such as annual or monthly measurements of national energy demand 
[37]. Space heating and cooling demand represent a major share of total energy demand in 
buildings and are strongly correlated with outdoor temperatures, which are measured at 
high spatiotemporal resolution (e.g. 0.5 grid cells and hourly recordings). Temperature data 
can be used to calculate heating and cooling degree days (HDDs and CDDs), which measure 
how much (in C) and for how long (in days) the outdoor air temperature was higher or lower 
than a given threshold temperature. HDDs and CDDs can then be used to derive a synthetic 
load profile for each location at any given time (e.g. all grid cells in the UK for each day of 
the year). These synthetic load profiles can then be we ighted according to the population 
contained in each cell, to account for the higher demand experienced in more populous 
regions, and scaled according to aggregate energy demand (e.g. annual UK building energy 
demand).  
 
The advantage of the degree -day method, compared with other methods, is that it can be 
applied in almost any region using only widely available data, thus satisfying our first and 
second criteria. Berger and Worlitschek [38] derive daily residential heating profiles for 
Switzerland using 30-year average temperature data. Sachs et al. [39] derive hourly heating 
and cooling profiles for four seasonally-representative days (i.e. 96 time-slices) across 165 
countries using hourly temperature data. The Hot Maps Project [40] estimate daily heating 
and cooling profiles for residential and tertiary consumers in all NUTS2 regions of the 
European Union using daily temperature data. When2Heat [41] estimate daily heating 
profiles for 16 European countries using temperature data and wind speeds. Despite several 
advances, a recent study shows that the model in [41] performs only marginally better than 
basic degree-day models when validated against UK gas demand [42]. In both [40] and [41], 
daily profiles are resolved further to hourly profile s using hourly demand factors derived 
from the German standard load profile [43]. Degree-day models are also used by industry. 
For example, the National Grid use this approach to forecast gas demand in the UK [44]. As 
with [41], their composite weather variable is derived from outdoor temperatures and wind 
speeds.  
 
Despite their broad applications, degree -day models generally fall short on our third and 
fourth criteria, as their results are can only be customised in limited ways (e.g. by varying 
threshold temperatures) and they are not extensively validated. Both physical and statistical 
models show that the physical properties of buildings, occupant dynamics and other 
meteorological variables can have a significant impact on energy consumption, yet t hese 
factors are rarely considered when deriving synthetic load profiles [5,24]. As a result, 
simulated load profiles typically show low geographical variation, which is compounded by 
the smoothing effect incurred when aggregating large numbers of buildi ngs [40,45,46]. In

[40] and [41] the authors apply different parameters to differentiate between building types, 
but they do not account for country -specific building dynamics or activity patterns as all 
hourly profiles are derived from German standard load profiles. In [41] and [44] the authors 
incorporate the effects of wind speeds into their profiles, but exclude other potentially 
influential meteorological variables (i.e. humidity and solar irradiance). The exclusion of 
these physical, behavioural, and meteorological factors precludes a customisable approach 
to modelling heating and cooling loads, in which a user can change input parameters to 
reflect the dynamics of an individual building or occupant. More importantly, existing studies 
make little attempt to validate their results against measured data relative to the physical 
and statistical models described previously. In [38] and [39] profiles are not validated against 
any temporally-resolved measurements. In [40] simulated profiles are validated only for 
several of the 16 countries, though results of this validation and the countries included are 
not documented. In [41] outputs are validated for the UK and Germany and, given its scope 
and application, outputs from [44] are validated extensively, but only for the UK.

Supplementary Note 5: Previous works 
Supplementary Table 1 summarises the advantages and disadvantages of each class of 
model discussed above. We present the Demand.ninja model which builds upon degree-day 
modelling, incorporating elements of physical and statistical modelling. D egree-day 
modelling is used as the foundation as it meets the two most important criteria for a 
universal model of heating and cooling demand: 1) it requires only limited and widely 
available data, and 2) it can be generalised to any geographical location.  We incorporate 
aspects of physical and statistical modelling to allow the user to customise or optimise 
simulations without ballooning the number of inputs. We then put extensive effort into 
validating our profiles against real -world data at the national,  regional, and individual -
building level; and develop representative parameters to allow for generalised use in any 
context. In the following paragraphs, we discuss the ways in which our approach builds on 
existing degree-day studies.  
 
HDDs and CDDs are the foundation of degree-day models. They have been estimated in many 
different studies, with resources range from academic studies [47–50] and databases [51–
53] to online calculators [54,55], with varied spatial and temporal resolution and coverage. 
One commonality among resources is that HDDs and CDDs are typically calculated using 
raw temperature data and fixed (often arbitrary) threshold temperatures. This simplistic 
approach runs contrary to evidence from statistical models that suggests that the inclusion 
of a range of meteorological variables can improve the accuracy of predictions, and that the 
heating and cooling requirements of occupants vary depending on geographical, cultural, 
economic and personal contexts, alongside the physical properties of buildings [5,19,23,24].  
 
In a recent study, Atalla, Gualdi and Lanza [51] produced a global database of HDDs and 
CDDs using three thermal indices (the Environmental Stress Index [56], Heat Index [57] and 
Humidex [58]) and three threshold temperatures (15.6, 18.3 and 21.1 °C). While their work 
represents a notable step forw ard, the database has three key limitations: 1) the indices 
used do not capture the effect of wind chill (only humidity and/or solar irradiance), 2) the 
threshold temperatures chosen are not tailored to specific contexts, and 3) their 
calculations do not account for the thermal inertia of buildings by smoothing temperatures 
such as in [41,44,59]. 
 
Thermal indices are used by meteorological agencies to better reflect the temperature that 
it ‘feels like’ to people. These indices can be applied as a proxy fo r the effects of solar 
irradiance, wind speed and/or humidity on heating and cooling demand. For example, a 
building will require more heating on days with lower solar radiation (due to reduced solar 
gains) or higher wind speeds (due to more air changes pe r hour). These cases would be 
represented by a lower value in the temperature index, if all else is held constant [44,51]. 
While there are many thermal indices, few include all potentially influential meteorological 
variables. For example, the Environmental Stress Index [56] accounts for just humidity and

8 
solar irradiance, while the Heat Index [57] and Humidex [58] account only for humidity. 
National Grid ESO developed the composite weather variable [44], used to forecast gas 
demand, which account s for the effects of temperature and wind speed. This is a better 
predictor of gas demand than raw temperature, but the omission of solar irradiance means 
that bespoke correction factors are required for spring and autumn. In a recent study, 
Copernicus [60] produced a state-of-the-art bioclimatology database (ERA5-HEAT), which 
includes the effects of solar irradiance, humidity and wind speeds. Their Universal Thermal 
Climate Index is defined as “the air temperature of a reference outdoor environment that 
would elicit in the human body the same physiological model’s response … as the actual 
environment”. We derive a similar index – the Building-Adjusted Internal Temperature (BAIT) 
– which accounts for the same factors but is optimised to the response of buildings (in terms 
of energy consumption), rather than human bodies (in terms of perception). This 
optimisation is pivotal in tailoring our index to the built environment, as meteorological 
conditions modify the perceived temperature differe ntly indoors (e.g. through solar gains, 
insulation and ventilation) than they do outdoors. 
 
Dynamic temperature smoothing and threshold temperatures can be used as proxies for the 
physical and behavioural factors that influence heating and cooling demand. Temperature 
smoothing (i.e. taking a weighted average over previous days) is designed to capture the 
thermal inertia of buildings, with smoother temperatures representing buildings with better 
insulation (i.e. slower heat transfer between the indoor and ou tdoor environment). This 
approach is widely employed in the gas industry as it was found to improve the correlation 
between degree days and measured gas consumption [44,61], and has more recently been 
applied in academic studies [41,59]. Similarly, thr eshold temperatures can be used to 
capture highly individual preferences in users’ demand for heating and cooling. For example, 
a higher threshold temperature for heating would be used to represent occupants with 
reduced tolerance to feeling cold, which may occur for economic reasons (greater affluence 
or lower energy costs), health reasons (e.g. a hospital or care home) and so on. A recent 
study [62] found that the average threshold temperature varies substantially across 
European countries. Allowing smo othing factors and threshold temperatures to be 
customised or optimised to fit a building or occupant helps to address the smoothing effect 
inherent when aggregating the heating and cooling profiles of many buildings.

Supplementary Note 6: Model simplifications 
The approach described in ‘Calculating degree days’  relies on four simplifications. First, 
outdoor temperatures vary throughout the day, but diurnal variations are not explicitly 
accounted for because HDDs and CDDs are calculated using daily avera ge, rather than 
hourly temperatures. For example, in temperate climates the daily average outdoor 
temperature may be higher than the threshold temperature, hence the model computes zero 
HDDs for that day, even though outdoor temperature falls below the threshold temperature 
for at least some portion of the night. The thermal inertia of buildings (represented in 
Equation 3 of the Methods) tends to an average of around 0.50, implying that past 
meteorological conditions are influential with a half-life of 24 hours. Small, short excursions 
below the heating threshold temperature overnight would therefore not reduce internal 
temperatures sufficiently to require heating. Hence despite this simplification, we find that 
the Demand.ninja performs comparably across al l climates and seasons (see Figure 4, 
Supplementary Figures 46–99). 
 
Second, with the metered energy demand data that are available it is not possible to isolate 
the energy consumed for space hea ting and cooling demand from other end uses. For 
example, domestic refrigerators will consume more electricity in summer in regions without 
air conditioning as internal room temperatures are higher, and this will be captured within 
our estimates of demand for space cooling. Isolating such effects would require more 
disaggregated smart-metering data than is currently available; however, from the datasets 
which do give appliance - or circuit-level granularity, we conclude that space heating and 
cooling are by far the largest sources of temperature-dependent demand in buildings. 
 
Third, when creating national and regional aggregated profiles, weighting the grid cells 
within a region based on population density implies that people only demand heating and 
cooling where they live and does not account for non -residential buildings, or people’s 
movements throughout the day. For example, facilities such as supermarkets and data 
centres have substantial cooling demand concentrated in specific areas that may not be 
highly populated, but will be attributed to the location of households in this approach. 
Similarly, the number of people in suburbs may decrease during working hours and the 
number in city centres may increase. This could be improved upon if granular data on 
industry locations with global scope, and time -varying global gridded data on human 
locations became openly available. 
 
Finally, the approach to aggregation assumes that all people within a country or region have 
the same desire for heating and cooling (and so if they experienced the same weather 
conditions, they would demand the same amount of energy). To improve upon this, t he 
population density in each grid cell was raised to the power of 0.8 to account for households 
typically having a smaller floor area in high-density cities [39].

The approach described in ‘Resolving profiles to hourly resolution’  relies on two 
simplifications. First, we assume that the diurnal profile for heating and cooling remain 
constant throughout the year, unlike the profile for baseline demand which is specific to the 
season and working vs. non -working days. For example, in New South Wales 
(Supplementary Figure 170) it can be seen that heating demand takes a similar shape during 
weekdays and weekends in winter and shoulder months, but takes a different shape during 
summer months. However, this is reasonably inconsequential as the coldest days in the 
Australian summer require little heating. 
 
Secondly, we assume that the diurnal profiles for heating and cooling are consistent around 
the world.  This is not a perfect approximation, but it holds for countries with a materially -
significant demand for heating and/or cooling. For example, the diurnal profiles for cooling 
across the Australian states, Japan and regions of the US (Supplementary Figure 207) follow 
a characteristic shape, with a peak around 3 PM and a nadir between 4 -6 AM. Cooling 
profiles in southern European countries (Spain, Italy and Greece) are similar, but with less 
pronounced variation, as these countries are not as h ot; but profiles in northern European 
countries (Germany, Britain) take a flatter shape. As their demand for cooling is an order of 
magnitude lower, this different shape has limited impact on the global weighted average. 
The heating demand profiles are ele vated during daytime hours with a small evening peak 
across many European nations and Australian states, but in Japan and the US regions they 
take a stronger diurnal profile that is flattened during peak sunlight hours (inverse to 
cooling).

Supplementary Note 7: Validation for other models 
In Figure 4, the Demand.ninja model is compared to three other models from the literature, 
as shown in Supplementary Figures 100–153. The estimates for space heating and cooling 
demand from these models were substituted into Equation 11 from Methods in place of HDD 
and CDD, and a linear regression against metered demand was used to find the intercept 
(baseline of temperature independent demand), scale factors for heating and  cooling 
demand (analogous to the power coefficients), and time -varying terms for non -working 
days and long -term trends. The coefficients for scaling each dataset are given in 
Supplementary Tables 5–7. 
 
For When2Heat [63], demand was estimated from the ‘heat_demand_total’ variable. As this 
model only provides heat demand, the validation was limited to those countries in Europe 
with limited demand for cooling (fewer than 5% as many CDD as HDD), and individual days 
with more than 1 CDD were removed from the comparison.  
 
For HotMaps [40], demand was estimated from the ‘load profile residential shw and heating 
yearlong 2010’ and ‘load profile residential cooling yearlong 2010’ datasets.  
 
NREL’s ResStock and ComStock models [64] provide estimates of total electricity demand, 
including thermal and non -thermal components. These demand estimates were still run 
through the regression described above for the sake of fairness, as this could only improve 
the potential goodness of fit. F or each model, energy demand is simulated using specific 
weather years rather than Typical Meteorological Years, ensuring a fair comparison across 
models.

Supplementary Note 8: Model calibration 
In total we calibrate the model in 43 regions and 4,773 individual buildings across the world 
using daily energy demand data, and 78 regions using monthly energy demand data.  The 
spread of results ( summarised in Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Figures 43 –
44) reflects the diversity of building types, living standards and occupant behaviours. 
 
Averaged a cross the sample of regions and years that we consider, 7.5% of electricity 
demand is attributed to heating and 6.3% to cooling. Daily electricity demand increases by 
2.6% for every degree that temperature falls below a country’s heating threshold, and by 
3.7% for every degree above the coolin g threshold. Japan is reasonably central among 
countries; its average electricity demand is 69 GW, and this increases by 2.0 GW for each 
degree that the BAIT falls below 11.5°C, and increases by 2.5 GW for every degree above 
20.1°C. After accounting for di fferences in population and climate, the US consumes the 
greatest amounts of electricity for both heating and cooling, while European countries 
consume considerably less. On average, US states also consume considerably more gas for 
heating than European co untries. Across regions, heating thresholds range from 9.7 to 
15.2°C, which is similar to the average thresholds derived for Europe in [62] (i.e. 13.4 and 
15.0°C for electric and gas heating respectively). Cooling thresholds derived in this study 
vary by just 1.6°C (from 18.8 to 20.4°C), which is similar to that reported in [19] (i.e. 19.1 ± 
3.9°C).  
 
Across individual buildings we find an optimal smoothing factor of around 0.50. This is the 
same as the smoothing factor used in [41], except that temperatures are smoothed over the 
three previous days rather than the two days considered here. Elexon (a British electricity 
market operator) use a smoothing factor of 0.57, with temperatures smoothed over the 
previous two days [61]. The smoothing factor do es, however, vary considerably across 
building datasets. For example, buildings in New Zealand have a low smoothing factor, 
suggesting they are less-well insulated or more frequently ventilated. Many houses in New 
Zealand are built from wood which has poor  insulating properties thereby supporting this 
inference. In Great Britain, the smoothing factor is many times higher owing to brick -built 
construction being ubiquitous, and highest in electrically-heated buildings, which are more 
likely to be flats (where  apartments that are adjacent, above or beneath will provide 
additional thermal moderation) and built according to more recent and stringent building 
standards (due to changes in construction demographics).  
 
The solar, wind and humidity coefficients all v ary considerably both within and across 
building datasets. Buildings with higher solar coefficients experience greater solar gains. 
Solar gains increase with the amount of sunlight that reaches the inside of the building, 
which in turn depends on the propo rtion of the building that is covered in windows, the 
orientation of the house and the shading of the house (e.g. by trees or other buildings). The 
wind coefficient becomes more negative as buildings become draughtier and more 

========== PAGE 14 ==========
13 
ventilated, which in turn dep ends on the exposure of the building, the airtightness of the 
envelope and occupant behaviour (keeping windows open during cold weather). The 
humidity coefficient increases as buildings are more exposed to humid air outside of the 
building, which again dep ends on the airtightness of the building envelope. Buildings are 
heterogenous, in terms of their age, style, orientation, fabric efficiency, heating system, 
users, and so forth, and so it is not possible to characterise the right parameters for a given 
‘type’ of dwelling (e.g. brick-built terraces in the UK or timber detached houses in the US). 
Even for individual buildings it is not possible to concretely determine the key driver of 
variation in solar, wind and humidity coefficients, because the building d atasets that are 
available to us provide insufficient metadata (in most cases nothing beyond the building’s 
approximate location) due to the costs and confidentiality issues of gathering more detailed 
data. In the absence of more detailed datasets for cali bration, users can use the generic 
parameters of the model, which have been shown to accurately simulate out -of-sample 
demand, or subjectively consider the range of parameters we found across large 
populations of diverse buildings to select the parameters that best fit the building they seek 
to simulate. For example, a higher solar coefficient could be used to model a building with 
a larger glazed area and a lower smoothing coefficient could be used to model a poorly 
insulated building.
